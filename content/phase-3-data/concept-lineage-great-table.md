# Concept Lineage: The Great Table
## From Dee's Reception to Modern Practice

---

## Narrative Summary

The Great Table of Earth — a 25-column by 27-row grid of letters encoding the names of angels, governors, and elemental hierarchies — is the most widely used structural artifact in the Enochian system, and also the most transformed. Its journey from Dee's study to modern magical practice passes through at least seven distinct hands, and at several of those handoffs, the object changed.

◆ Dee received the Great Table during the Cracow sessions (c. 1584), dictated letter by letter through Kelley's scrying. He recorded it in Sloane MS 3191. The table as Dee recorded it assigns specific elemental qualities to its four quadrants, though the exact nature of Dee's original elemental assignments is itself a matter of scholarly discussion (? — Peterson and Leitch have proposed different reconstructions of Dee's intended arrangement).

◆ After the traumatic disruption of 1587, Dee received instructions to "reform" the table — a revised version with specific letter changes. The Reformed Table (sometimes called the Tabula Recensa) exists in Dee's later manuscript notes, but the relationship between the original and the reform is not fully clear (~ — did the reform supersede the original, or were both intended to be in use?).

◆ The manuscripts passed through several hands after Dee's death in 1608. Elias Ashmole acquired a significant portion of the Dee papers in the mid-17th century; they eventually entered the Sloane collection at what became the British Library.

△ Méric Casaubon published *A True & Faithful Relation* in 1659, making the session records — though not the Great Table itself in full grid form — available to a wider audience. Casaubon's editorial framing (demonic deception) shaped how the material was received for two centuries.

△ In the late 19th century, S.L. MacGregor Mathers and the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn reorganized the Great Table. They reassigned the elemental quarters (changing which element corresponds to which quadrant), introduced the Tablet of Union as a central coordinating device, and added Hebrew letter correspondences not present in Dee's original. The exact reasoning behind Mathers' changes has never been fully explained in surviving documents (? — scholarly debate exists on whether Mathers was correcting what he perceived as errors, adapting the system to the Golden Dawn's broader framework, or working from a different manuscript source).

△ Israel Regardie published the Golden Dawn's version of the Great Table in *The Golden Dawn* (1937–1940). For the vast majority of 20th- and 21st-century Enochian practitioners, Regardie's publication *is* the Great Table. The Golden Dawn arrangement, not Dee's original, is what most people encounter when they study Enochian material.

◇ In the late 20th and early 21st centuries, scholars including Joseph Peterson, Donald Laycock, and Aaron Leitch returned to the primary manuscripts and produced transcriptions and analyses of Dee's original table. Peterson's digital editions (esotericarchives.com) made the manuscript evidence accessible for the first time to readers without access to the British Library. The scholarly reconstruction of Dee's original arrangement differs from the Golden Dawn version in the assignment of elemental quarters and in numerous individual cell readings.

The Great Table that most people know is not the Great Table that Dee recorded. The Observatory exists to make that visible — not as an accusation of corruption, but as a fact of transmission.

## Key Divergences

### Divergence 1: The Golden Dawn Rearrangement (c. 1888–1900)

**Node:** tn-person-mathers / tn-inst-golden-dawn

**Description:** Mathers reorganized the Great Table's elemental quarters, changing which element corresponds to which quadrant. In Dee's arrangement (as reconstructed by modern scholars), the quarters have one set of elemental assignments. In the Golden Dawn version, these assignments are different. Mathers also introduced the Tablet of Union — a 4×5 grid of letters drawn from the Great Table's central cross — as a separate ritual object. The Tablet of Union does not exist as a separate entity in Dee's manuscripts.

**Branches:**
1. **Dee's arrangement** (tradition: null) — The Great Table as recorded in Sloane 3191. Continued in modern scholarly reconstructions (Peterson, Laycock).
2. **Golden Dawn arrangement** (tradition: Golden Dawn) — The rearranged table with modified elemental quarters and the Tablet of Union. Continued through Regardie's publication into mainstream Western occultism.

**Uncertainty:** probable — The divergence itself is well-documented. The *reason* for it is uncertain.

- ~ Is the Golden Dawn's elemental quarter assignment an "error," a "correction," or a deliberate magical adaptation? The answer depends on one's framework — historical scholarship treats it as a modification; practicing Golden Dawn members may treat it as a received correction.
