## How to Read This Document

### Epistemic Badge Key (canonical)

| Badge | Meaning |
|:---:|:---|
| **◆** | Historical Evidence — attested in primary documents; scholarly consensus on factuality |
| **◇** | Strong Scholarly Consensus — not a raw document, but a reading that virtually all serious modern scholars accept |
| **○** | Traditional Occult Claim — held within the tradition as truth; not historically provable |
| **△** | Later Interpretation — added by commentators, revivalists, or modern readers |
| **◎** | Parallel — structural resemblance to another system; not causal |
| **~** | Speculative — plausible but unconfirmed |
| **?** | Disputed / Contested Claim — active scholarly disagreement |
| **⚠** | Caution — potentially destabilizing; frame carefully |

### Confidence Scale (for individual source-to-claim links)

- **High** — multiple independent sources, primary-document grounding
- **Moderate** — single strong source, consistent with adjacent evidence
- **Low** — late, hostile, antiquarian, or otherwise filtered
- **Contested** — sources disagree at the surface level

### Lens Assignment

Every source is tagged for the interpretive lens in which it is most load-bearing:

- **H** — Historical Lens
- **O** — Traditional Occult Lens
- **P** — Psychological Lens
- **S** — Speculative Lens
- **M** — Irreducible Mixture (operates across lenses)

### Rank-Visibility Key

- **S1 (Seeker)** — surfaces in the lesson body or "Who Says This?"
- **S2 (Student)** — surfaces in the glossary source notes
- **S3 (Observer)** — surfaces in the full archive figure entry
- **S4 (Interpreter)** — surfaces in the comparative apparatus
- **S5 (Cartographer)** — surfaces in the cross-reference infrastructure
- **S6 (Adept)** — surfaces in the full critical apparatus
- **S7 (Archivist)** — full editorial access, including unresolved items

## I. Primary Manuscript Sources

### 1. Sloane MS 3188 — *Mysteriorum Libri Quinti* (Five Books of Mystery)

**Repository:** British Library, Department of Manuscripts, Sloane Collection
**Shelfmark:** Sloane MS 3188
**Dating:** Session records 1581/82–1583; compiled and bound by Dee during his lifetime
**Author:** John Dee (holograph, principal hand) with session transcripts from Edward Kelley's dictation
**Physical description:** Paper codex; Dee's mixed Latin-English diary hand; marginalia; diagrams
**Languages:** Latin, English, occasional Greek and Enochian

**Supports these claims in Lesson 1.1:**
- The existence and dating of the scrying sessions beginning in 1581–82 — **◆ High, Lens: H**
- The Heptarchic system (49 angelic kings and princes, Sigillum Dei Aemeth, Holy Table, Lamen) dictated in 1582 — **◆ High, Lens: H**
- The division of labor: Kelley scries, Dee records — **◆ High, Lens: H**
- Kelley's arrival under the name "Edward Talbot" — **◆ High, Lens: H**
- Barnabas Saul as Dee's prior scryer — **◆ High, Lens: H**

**Editions consulted:**
- Peterson, Joseph H. *John Dee's Five Books of Mystery* (2003) — the standard modern edition with English translation of the Latin and folio concordance.

**Rank visibility:** S1 onward

## II. Early Printed Sources

### 5. Casaubon, Meric — *A True & Faithful Relation of What Passed for Many Years Between Dr. John Dee and Some Spirits* (London, 1659)

**Publisher:** Printed by D. Maxwell for T. Garthwait, London, 1659
**Editor:** Meric Casaubon (1599–1671), classical scholar, son of Isaac Casaubon, Anglican clergyman, hostile witness
**Source text:** Casaubon published selections from Dee's spiritual diaries, chiefly the later Continental sessions from 1583 onward. He did **not** have access to the Mortlake *Mysteriorum Libri Quinti* (Sloane MS 3188).

**Supports these claims in Lesson 1.1:**
- The publication event itself (Section V of the lesson): Casaubon published in 1659, making diaries publicly available for the first time — **◆ High, Lens: H**
- Casaubon's intent was cautionary ("a warning against enthusiasm") — **◆ High, Lens: H**
- The Continental session material as transmitted — **◆ High for what Dee wrote; ⚠ Cautionary for Casaubon's hostile editorial framing**

**The Casaubon Bias:** Lesson 1.1 correctly notes that Casaubon "intended it as a cautionary tale. Others read it differently." This framing is appropriate for Seeker rank. The full apparatus of Casaubon's rhetorical strategy surfaces at S3 and S4.

**Rank visibility:** S1 onward

## III. Modern Scholarly Sources

### 6. Woolley, Benjamin — *The Queen's Conjurer: The Science and Magic of Dr. John Dee* (Henry Holt, 2001)

**Character:** Popular narrative history of Dee's life. Readable, well-researched for a trade press book, but not a primary scholarly monograph. Cite with care.
**Relevance to Lesson 1.1:** Woolley provides the most accessible narrative of Dee's library, his role advising Elizabeth I, and the cultural milieu of Elizabethan learned magic. Several framing claims in the lesson (Dee's advisory role, the lecture on Euclid in Paris, the imprisonment under Mary) are consistent with Woolley's account.
**Lens:** H
**Rank visibility:** S3

### 7. Harkness, Deborah E. — *John Dee's Conversations with Angels: Cabala, Alchemy, and the End of Nature* (Cambridge University Press, 1999)

**Character:** The standard modern academic treatment. Harkness reads the angelic sessions as a coherent natural-philosophical project, situating them within late-Renaissance intellectual culture.
**Relevance to Lesson 1.1:** Load-bearing for the intellectual context: Dee's exhaustion of natural-scientific avenues, his turn to scrying, his sincere belief in angelic communication as a form of natural philosophical inquiry. Harkness is the strongest scholarly support for the lesson's framing of Dee as neither credulous nor marginal.
**Lens:** H (primary), P (secondary)
**Rank visibility:** S1 onward

### 8. Peterson, Joseph H. — *John Dee's Five Books of Mystery: Original Sourcebook of Enochian Magic* (Weiser Books, 2003)

**Character:** Critical edition of Sloane MS 3188 with English translation of Latin portions, apparatus, and introduction. Peterson also maintains *Twilit Grotto: Esoteric Archives* (esotericarchives.com).
**Relevance to Lesson 1.1:** Peterson is the practical working edition for all session dates and content from the Mortlake period cited in the lesson. The standard citation bridge between "Sloane MS 3188" and a readable English page.
**Lens:** H
**Rank visibility:** S1 onward

### 9. Parry, Glyn — *The Arch-Conjuror of England: John Dee* (Yale University Press, 2011)

**Character:** A political biography of Dee emphasizing his court connections, intelligence work, and political maneuvering. Stronger on Dee's public career than on the angelic sessions.
**Relevance to Lesson 1.1:** Supports the claims about Dee's advisory role to Elizabeth I, his imprisonment under Mary, and his lecture on Euclid in Paris. Parry treats Dee as a political actor, not merely a scholar.
**Lens:** H
**Rank visibility:** S3

### 10. Clulee, Nicholas H. — *John Dee's Natural Philosophy: Between Science and Religion* (Routledge, 1988)

**Character:** The earlier standard monograph on Dee's intellectual formation. Less focused on the angelic sessions but essential for the pre-Kelley Dee.
**Relevance to Lesson 1.1:** Supports the claim that Dee worked across mathematics, cartography, optics, and astronomy. Clulee is the strongest source for Dee's intellectual range and for the *Monas Hieroglyphica* (1564) as a bridge between natural philosophy and the occult.
**Lens:** H
**Rank visibility:** S3

### 11. French, Peter J. — *John Dee: The World of an Elizabethan Magus* (Routledge, 1972)

**Character:** Older but still widely cited narrative biography. Somewhat romanticized. Useful as a counterweight to Harkness's naturalization of Dee.
**Relevance to Lesson 1.1:** General biographical framing. French's narrative of Dee at Mortlake and the turn to scrying is consistent with the lesson's account.
**Lens:** H
**Rank visibility:** S3

### 12. Roberts, Julian, and Watson, Andrew G. — *John Dee's Library Catalogue* (Bibliographical Society, 1990)

**Character:** The definitive scholarly catalogue of Dee's library based on his own manuscript catalogue and surviving volumes. This is the primary reference for all library-size claims.
**Relevance to Lesson 1.1:** Critical for the library-size claim. See Badge Audit (Section VI) for a detailed discussion.
**On the numbers:** Roberts and Watson's catalogue identifies approximately 2,292 titles that can be positively attributed to Dee's library from his own catalogue (compiled c. 1583). However, the catalogue is known to be incomplete — Dee did not list everything, and significant portions of the library were dispersed or destroyed during the 1583 raid on Mortlake while Dee was abroad. Estimates of the total collection, accounting for unrecorded volumes, range upward. The lesson's claim of "over 3,000 volumes" falls within the range of scholarly estimates but is above the documented count.
**Lens:** H
**Rank visibility:** S2 (for the catalogue as source); S4 (for the counting methodology)

### 13. Asprem, Egil — *Arguing with Angels: Enochian Magic and Modern Occulture* (State University of New York Press, 2012)

**Character:** The standard modern study of the *reception* of Enochian magic: Golden Dawn, Crowley, post-war occultism, contemporary practice.
**Relevance to Lesson 1.1:** Asprem underwrites the lesson's treatment of Section V (the three moments of survival: Casaubon, Golden Dawn, Crowley). He is also the strongest scholarly support for the lesson's epistemic posture — the insistence that description and interpretation be kept apart.
**On the Golden Dawn modifications:** Asprem documents that the Golden Dawn added elemental attributions to the Watchtowers, mapped the system onto the Kabbalistic Tree of Life, and developed ritual procedures absent from Dee's originals. The lesson's distinction between ◆ (for the fact of integration) and △ (for the modifications) is consistent with Asprem's analysis.
**Lens:** H, M
**Rank visibility:** S1 onward

### 14. Laycock, Donald C. — *The Complete Enochian Dictionary* (1978; revised edition, Samuel Weiser, 1994)

**Character:** The standard linguistic reference for the Enochian language. Laycock attempted a systematic analysis of Enochian vocabulary and grammar. His own conclusion was that Enochian is not a natural language but shows more internal consistency than a simple cipher.
**Relevance to Lesson 1.1:** The lesson's claims about the Calls having "internal linguistic consistency" and a "vocabulary of approximately 1,000 words" are consistent with Laycock's analysis. The "approximately 1,000 words" figure derives from Laycock's lexicon.
**Lens:** H, S
**Rank visibility:** S3

## IV. Comparative / Parallel Sources

Lesson 1.1 makes extensive reference to traditions of lost language and hidden knowledge. These are not presented as sources Dee copied from (though he knew some of them); they are presented as a cultural pattern within which the Enochian project becomes intelligible. Each reference needs a source entry.

### 15. Genesis / The Babel Narrative (Genesis 11:1-9)

**Text:** The Hebrew Bible's account of the confusion of tongues at Babel.
**Status in the lesson:** Referenced in the Opening Sequence and Section I as one instance of the "lost language" pattern. The lesson states that the Hebrew Bible "describes the confusion of tongues at Babel as divine punishment for human arrogance."
**Badge:** ◆ for the existence and content of the Genesis text. The narrative itself is a religious text, not a historical claim about an actual event.
**Editions:** Any standard critical edition of the Hebrew Bible (e.g., *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*). For the English, NRSV or KJV.
**Confidence:** High (that this text exists, says what the lesson says it says, and was known to Dee)
**Lens:** H (for the text's existence and influence), O (for its theological reading)
**Rank visibility:** S1

### 16. Sefer Yetzirah (Book of Formation/Creation)

**Text:** Early Jewish cosmogonic-linguistic text, widely attributed in the tradition to the patriarch Abraham but of uncertain authorship.
**Dating:** The lesson states "2nd and 6th centuries CE." This is consistent with scholarly consensus. Hayman (2004) argues for a date no later than the 3rd century CE; Liebes (2000) and others have proposed later dates. The range of 2nd-6th centuries CE is a responsible summary of the field. See Hayman, A. Peter. *Sefer Yesira: Edition, Translation and Text-Critical Commentary* (Mohr Siebeck, 2004).
**Status in the lesson:** Quoted ("With thirty-two wondrous paths of wisdom... He created His universe") and described as widely influential in Dee's intellectual environment.
**Badge:** ◇ for the dating range. ◆ for the text's existence and its claim that letters are instruments of creation. The quotation is from Sefer Yetzirah 1:1, widely available in multiple translations.
**Confidence:** High (the text exists and says what the lesson says); Moderate (for the precise dating range, which remains debated but the stated range is defensible)
**Lens:** H, O
**Rank visibility:** S1 (quotation and dating); S4 (full comparative apparatus)

### 17. 1 Enoch (Book of Enoch / Ethiopic Enoch)

**Text:** Pseudepigraphical Jewish apocalyptic text, preserved in full only in Ge'ez (Ethiopic). Contains the Book of the Watchers, the Book of Parables, the Astronomical Book, the Dream Visions, and the Epistle of Enoch.
**Status in the lesson:** Not directly quoted but implied in the broader "lost knowledge" framing and in the system's name ("Enochian"). The connection between the patriarch Enoch as a receiver of heavenly knowledge and Dee's claim to receive angelic transmissions is structural.
**Badge:** ◎ Parallel — structural resemblance (a human receiving knowledge from heavenly beings), not a claim that Dee worked directly from 1 Enoch (though he may have known of the tradition).
**Standard edition:** Nickelsburg, George W. E., and VanderKam, James C. *1 Enoch: The Hermeneia Translation* (Fortress Press, 2012). Also: Charles, R. H. *The Book of Enoch* (1893; reprinted frequently).
**Confidence:** High (for the parallel as structural pattern)
**Lens:** H, O
**Rank visibility:** S1 (named); S4 (full parallel apparatus)

### 18. Gnostic Texts (Nag Hammadi Library)

**Text:** A collection of early Christian and Gnostic texts discovered at Nag Hammadi, Egypt, in 1945. The lesson references the Gnostic concept of *gnosis* — a spark of divine knowledge trapped in matter.
**Status in the lesson:** Named in Section I as one instance of the "lost knowledge" pattern. The lesson states: "The Gnostic traditions describe a spark of divine knowledge — *gnosis* — trapped in matter, awaiting recovery."
**Badge:** ◆ for the existence of Gnostic traditions making this claim. The description is a responsible summary of a central Gnostic motif (particularly in Valentinian and Sethian texts).
**Standard edition:** Robinson, James M., ed. *The Nag Hammadi Library in English* (3rd ed., HarperSanFrancisco, 1990). Also: Meyer, Marvin, ed. *The Nag Hammadi Scriptures* (HarperOne, 2007).
**Note on anachronism:** The Nag Hammadi texts were discovered in 1945; Dee could not have known them. However, Gnostic ideas circulated in the Renaissance through Hermetic and Kabbalistic channels. The lesson uses "Gnostic traditions" as a general descriptor of a recurrent motif, not as a claim that Dee read specific Gnostic texts.
**Confidence:** High (for the tradition's existence and the accuracy of the description)
**Lens:** H, O
**Rank visibility:** S1 (named); S4 (comparative apparatus)

### 19. Hermetic Texts (Corpus Hermeticum)

**Text:** A body of texts attributed to Hermes Trismegistus (Thoth-Hermes), compiled in late antiquity (2nd-3rd centuries CE). The lesson states they "present themselves as remnants of a pristine ancient wisdom."
**Status in the lesson:** Named in Section I. Dee knew these texts intimately; the Hermetic tradition was a major pillar of Renaissance occult philosophy.
**Badge:** ◆ for the existence of the Hermetic corpus and its self-presentation as *prisca theologia*. ◆ for Dee's documented familiarity (attested through Roberts & Watson's library catalogue and through Dee's own writings).
**Standard edition:** Copenhaver, Brian P. *Hermetica: The Greek Corpus Hermeticum and the Latin Asclepius in a New English Translation* (Cambridge University Press, 1992).
**Confidence:** High
**Lens:** H, O
**Rank visibility:** S1 (named); S4 (comparative apparatus)

### 20. Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite

**Text:** A corpus of late-5th/early-6th-century CE Christian mystical writings, attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite (mentioned in Acts 17:34) but composed centuries later. Includes *The Celestial Hierarchy*, *The Divine Names*, and *The Mystical Theology*.
**Status in the lesson:** Not directly named in the lesson text but implied in the general framing of celestial hierarchies and angelic ordering. Dee's angelology draws on the Pseudo-Dionysian hierarchy (seraphim, cherubim, thrones, etc.) as mediated through medieval and Renaissance reception.
**Badge:** ◎ Parallel — structural resonance between Pseudo-Dionysian celestial hierarchy and the Enochian system's elaborate angelic ordering.
**Standard edition:** Luibheid, Colm, trans. *Pseudo-Dionysius: The Complete Works* (Paulist Press, 1987).
**Confidence:** High (for the parallel as structural pattern); Moderate (for Dee's direct engagement, which is attested through his library but not through explicit citation in the session diaries)
**Lens:** H, O
**Rank visibility:** S4

### 21. Plato — Anamnesis (Recollection)

**Text:** Plato's theory that all learning is the recovery of knowledge the soul already possesses, most fully articulated in the *Meno* and *Phaedo*.
**Status in the lesson:** Named in Section I: "Plato's theory of anamnesis suggests that all learning is the recovery of knowledge the soul already possesses but has forgotten."
**Badge:** ◆ for the existence of Plato's theory and the accuracy of the description.
**Standard edition:** Cooper, John M., ed. *Plato: Complete Works* (Hackett, 1997). The anamnesis doctrine appears primarily in *Meno* 80d-86c and *Phaedo* 72e-77a.
**Confidence:** High
**Lens:** H
**Rank visibility:** S1 (named); S4 (comparative apparatus)

### 22. Sumerian *Me*

**Text:** The lesson's Opening Sequence references the Sumerians and their concept of *me* — divine decrees or cosmic principles. The lesson states: "The Sumerians called the divine decrees *me* — the operating code of the cosmos."
**Status in the lesson:** A brief cultural reference in the Opening Sequence to establish the universality of the "lost language" impulse.
**Badge:** ◆ for the existence of the Sumerian concept of *me*. The description "operating code of the cosmos" is a modern interpretive gloss — ◇ at best — but is consistent with how Assyriology describes the concept.
**Standard reference:** Black, Jeremy, and Green, Anthony. *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia: An Illustrated Dictionary* (University of Texas Press, 1992). Also: Kramer, Samuel Noah. *The Sumerians: Their History, Culture, and Character* (University of Chicago Press, 1963).
**Confidence:** High (for the concept's existence); Moderate (for the "operating code" gloss, which is interpretive)
**Lens:** H
**Rank visibility:** S1 (named); S4 (comparative apparatus)

## VI. Badge Audit

### General Assessment

Lesson 1.1 uses badges conservatively. The overwhelming majority of factual claims carry ◆, which is appropriate: this is an orientation lesson grounded in what the documentary record contains. The lesson clearly labels ○ (Traditional Occult Claims) and △ (Later Interpretations) where they appear, and the Speculative Lens is explicitly marked ~.

### Specific Audit Notes

**Note A — "Polished obsidian mirror" (Claim #1)**

The lesson states that in 1581, Dee "sat down in front of a polished obsidian mirror and began to pray." This conflates two things that require separation:

1. **That Dee began scrying sessions in 1581:** ◆ High — attested in Sloane MS 3188.
2. **That the scrying instrument was a "polished obsidian mirror":** ? Contested. The obsidian mirror now in the British Museum (1966,1001.1) is traditionally attributed to Dee, but the attribution chain passes through Horace Walpole's 18th-century collecting. Dee's early sessions reference a "shew-stone" or "chrystalline globe." The diary entries for the earliest sessions do not specify an obsidian mirror. The crystal shew-stone (BM SLCups.232) has a stronger (though still not unbroken) provenance chain for the early Mortlake sessions.

**Recommendation:** Upgrade the specificity. The lesson should say "a polished stone" or "a scrying glass" rather than "a polished obsidian mirror" unless the claim is explicitly caveated. The current phrasing presents a contested attribution as settled fact.

**Precision-upgrade language:** "...sat down in front of a scrying stone — a polished surface meant to receive visions — and began to pray."

**Badge change:** The sentence should carry ◆ for the act of scrying, with a note that the specific instrument is ○/? (traditional attribution, not documented).

---

**Note B — "Largest private library in England — over 3,000 volumes" (Claim #2)**

Roberts and Watson's *John Dee's Library Catalogue* (1990) identifies approximately 2,292 titles positively attributable to Dee from his own catalogue (compiled c. 1583). This is the documented floor. However:

- The catalogue is known to be incomplete; Dee did not list every volume.
- Significant portions of the library were lost during the 1583 raid on Mortlake while Dee was abroad.
- Scholarly estimates of the total collection at its peak vary. Some estimates run as high as 4,000 volumes when accounting for unrecorded items and losses.
- The claim "largest private library in England" is widely repeated in the secondary literature (Woolley, French, Parry) and is consistent with what is known of contemporary private collections. Harkness and Roberts & Watson support it as a defensible characterization.

**Assessment:** "Over 3,000 volumes" is within the range of scholarly estimates but is above the documented count of ~2,292. It is not false, but it is not the most conservative statement available.

**Recommendation:** Either:
- (a) Retain "over 3,000 volumes" but add a ◇ badge and a "Who Says This?" entry noting the Roberts & Watson count and the estimation range, or
- (b) Revise to "perhaps 3,000 or more volumes" or "a collection that may have reached 3,000 volumes at its peak" — language that signals estimation rather than certainty.

The claim "largest private library in England" should carry ◇ (scholarly consensus characterization, not a precise documentary claim).

---

**Note C — "Four years" span (Claim #34)**

The lesson's Opening Sequence says the transmissions "would take four years." The primary transmission period runs from 1582 (Kelley's arrival) to 1587 (the partnership's effective end) — a span of approximately five years. Some transmissions continued sporadically after 1587, though the sustained collaborative work ended.

If "four years" refers specifically to the period of intense systematic reception (roughly 1583-1587 or 1584-1587), it is defensible. If it refers to the total span of the Dee-Kelley working relationship, "five years" or "approximately five years" would be more accurate. The lesson's own Section IV uses "Between 1582 and 1587," which is a five-year span.

**Recommendation:** Revise to "five years" or "approximately five years" for consistency with the lesson's own later dating, or specify "four years of intensive transmission" if the narrower period is intended.

---

**Note D — Badge Inflation Check**

No cases of badge inflation detected. The lesson does not promote ○ claims to ◆ status. The ○ (Occult Lens) and △ (Golden Dawn modifications) are correctly marked. The ~ (Speculative Lens) is correctly bounded.

**Note E — Badge Deflation Check**

One potential deflation: the lesson's closing Epistemic Status line says "◆ Historical Evidence for all factual claims." This is slightly overstated given the obsidian mirror and library-size issues identified above. The closing should read: "◆ Historical Evidence for the majority of factual claims; ◇ Scholarly Consensus where noted; ○ Traditional Occult Claims clearly labeled; △ Later Interpretations attributed; ~ Speculative material explicitly marked."

**Location:** Opening Sequence, paragraph 4
**Issue:** The "polished obsidian mirror" creates an iconic visual image that serves the lesson's composed opening well. However, it asserts a contested attribution as fact. **Decision needed:** Does the lesson prioritize the composed image (in which case the obsidian mirror stays, with a caveat added) or documentary precision (in which case "scrying stone" replaces it)? Both are defensible. The human should decide.
